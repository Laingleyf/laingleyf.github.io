The Real Truth About Many Neighborhoods in Syracuse

Hundreds of properties sit empty across the city's neighborhoods many ranging from commercial to residential. What does this have to say about the cities poverty issues?

By Laingley Freemyer ·  April 2026  ·  Newhouse School

Abandoned House in Syracuse Neighborhood  |  Photo credit: Spectrum News

Drive through Brighton, or the Northside, or the Near Westside on any given afternoon and you'll see them: boarded windows, sagging porches, overgrown lots where a house once stood. Syracuse has long carried the weight of vacant and abandoned properties. Now, a city dataset cataloguing more than 300 of these addresses offers a rare neighborhood-by-neighborhood window into the full scope of the problem.

The dataset contains more than 300 vacant properties spread across nearly every corner of the city. The overwhelming majority are classified as residential, meaning houses and multi-unit homes that once sheltered families now stand empty. There are multiple neighborhoods that carry this burden the most. Some highlighted are Brighton with 209 vacant properties, Northside with 149 and Near Westside with 98. These are not random geographic coincidences. They are the same neighborhoods that housing researchers and city officials have long identified as suffering from concentrated poverty and decades of disinvestment. Areas where the economic fallout of deindustrialization hit hardest and has lingered longest.

The data also reveals a pattern of vacant property holders. Anita Washington, with a listed address in Clay, NY, appears as the owner of 10 separate vacant properties, all of which residential, scattered across Brighton, Elmwood, the Northside, and the Near Westside. The addresses range from West Newell Street to Ostrander Avenue to Seward Street. Floyd Mitchell, who lists only a P.O. Box in Syracuse, appears as the owner of six vacant properties all also residential. These range in Brighton, Southside, and Southwest. These are just two examples of housing that could be used to solve homelessness in Syracuse. As the problem continues, so does homelessness in Syracuse. Additionally it is seen that Brighton and Southside are repeating neighborhoods being represented in this data set and that is because these neighborhoods have a very high poverty rate. According to Syracuse.com the homeless rate has doubled from 2022 to 2024. Rising 67% for family homelessness and 136% for child poverty. Home vacancy contributes to the instability of families and neighborhoods and is a major problem that needs to be solved.

While the vacancy problem is a majot issue in Syracuse, there are organzations out there trying to help. The Syracuse Land Bank is working to develop different neighborhoods across the Syracuse area. They are taking run down homes and partnering with different contractors to rebuild homes. This not only solves the vacancy problems of under developed neighborhoods, but also it is slowly solving the problem of poverty in Syracuse.
